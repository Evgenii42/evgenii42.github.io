About me · An unfolding story

I have always wanted to make a wider impact.

I started with engineering systems. Over time, I realised that what draws me in most is the people behind them and the chance to change something together.

Kostroma, St Petersburg and my first projects

I was born in Kostroma, Russia. I spent around eight years swimming and also played chess. Later, I moved to St Petersburg and completed a master’s degree at Polytechnic University.

I started working as an engineer in my third year at university. By my fourth year, I was taking on projects of my own, combining engineering with responsibility for managing the work. Ventilation, water treatment, dough-mixing machines and local automation: I worked with different industrial systems in companies both large and small.

I enjoyed understanding how things worked and seeing the results of my work in action. Alongside that came a growing desire to take part in decisions beyond an individual engineering task.

Gas turbines and the desire to improve how we work

At Gazprom avtomatizatsia, I worked on gas transportation systems and gas turbines. I travelled extensively across Russia, including beyond the Arctic Circle. On major construction projects, I worked as an automation engineer, the company’s senior engineer on site, and a team leader.

It was fascinating work: complex systems, aircraft turbines and responsibility on site. My colleagues and managers trusted me. I tried to introduce new approaches carefully and build relationships with customers that helped us work effectively together, even when the tasks and circumstances were demanding.

That was when I became interested in management. Reading the fifth edition of PMBOK, I was struck by how much management could influence the success of the work. I wanted to contribute to that. It frustrated me to see an opportunity for improvement without being able to change a process or bring an idea into a product.

I began studying English seriously. I wanted to take on a different scale of challenge, change my life and expand what I was capable of doing.

An international team and a move into management

My next step was Statistics & Control, an American company with a remote working model. I wanted experience with international customers and an international product.

I worked on oil and gas production and petrochemical projects and worked extensively with Honeywell equipment. In a team of four, we wrote scripts for reverse engineering and automating migration to newer equipment versions. That helped us handle a substantial workload.

I increasingly took responsibility for customer communication, solving problems and coordinating the team. Later, I moved into projects involving digital twins, forecasting, modelling and the OptiRamp product. I worked with major Russian customers as well as on international projects, and later became a project manager.

Learning to move forward

I wanted to understand business more deeply and work on products and global projects. I enrolled in the Master in Management programme at HSE and completed it with distinction. The programme helped me identify the direction I wanted to take.

Some time later, I moved to Kazakhstan and began learning at an intense pace. Preparing for and taking the PMP exam was a challenge in its own right. Alongside it, I read widely about management and marketing. Storynomics, Kotler, and the behavioural approaches of Fogg, Kahneman and Cialdini particularly interested me: how people make decisions, and why some stories resonate while others do not.

Startups and a different kind of uncertainty

After my work with Statistics & Control ended, I was exploring what to do next when I found a volunteer role at a startup incubator. There were many projects there. I joined one as a leader and product manager.

We worked together for only a couple of months, but they were exceptionally eventful for me. I learned more about building a project from scratch, fundraising and working with investors. I was reading voraciously again: The Lean Startup, The Mom Test, The Black Swan and many other books. I had found a new direction.

The project was moving forward, but my view of its future increasingly diverged from the founder’s. Eventually, I decided to leave. It was a difficult moment: after such an absorbing experience, I did not know where to put my energy next. I had ideas, but much less certainty.

The next morning, I woke up to a stream of messages. People I had worked with were suggesting that we keep going together.

EmTalk: when the team asked to keep going

In 2023, those conversations grew into EmTalk. Our first idea was a B2C app about mental well-being. My wife suggested looking at the user journey in a cycle and pregnancy tracking app she knew. That became a starting point for our own idea.

I took responsibility for strategy and became the main founder. We divided tasks, recruited graduates from training schools, researched competitors and gradually built a team. But our first serious difficulty came before the product: some information stayed with one participant, and for me that became a question of trust.

The original team broke apart. We agreed that everyone would have access to the work we had produced and could continue independently. I wanted to keep building EmTalk. Most of the team chose to stay with me. After the doubts I had felt when leaving the incubator, that mattered personally: people wanted to carry on with our shared work.

How we continued after the first team broke apart.

Making room for the next attempt

Later, I invited my wife and friends to join. We discussed roles and the possibility of sharing equity. We conducted customer interviews, found our first designer and developed early prototypes. I enjoyed working with uncertainty, but it became clear that we still had a lot to learn together.

We considered venture funding. When an offer of around $100,000 for 30% came up, we decided it was not the right step for us at that stage. We put venture funding on hold and focused on finding product-market fit: understanding whom we could help and what value we could offer.

Interaction, learning and participation became a bigger part of our work. I wanted to bring together people who could experiment, make mistakes and try again. Looking back, I see this as building a culture and community that became a foundation for our work on products.

Why we chose that path. The historical research from early 2024 also shows the questions we were asking and how we imagined the first versions of the product.

What drives me

If you ask what I do especially well, I would say working with people and stakeholders. Finding common ground, building trust and getting people interested in a challenge I believe in matter a great deal to me.

I think of it as a small spark: I work, talk about what excites me, and see other people become eager to act. To me, empathy is a big part of that. Those messages from the team were a particularly personal reminder of how much this part of the work matters to me.

I am driven by the chance to help shape decisions, try something new and see an idea become a shared endeavour.

This is not the whole story. EmTalk has taken many more turns. I am gradually sharing them here, connecting personal experience with product decisions.
